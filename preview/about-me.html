About Me

Founder, student, and the person who answers the support email.

I grew up in Las Vegas watching two people sell houses for a living, and what I actually learned from them had nothing to do with real estate. It was this: do the work in front of the customer, do it honestly, and let the record speak. I have been trying to build software the same way ever since.

Where I am from

Las Vegas — the real one, not the Strip. My parents, Tom and Serena Heuser, have been residents for twenty-seven years and run The Heuser Team, a two-person real estate practice in Summerlin with more than 976 closed transactions behind it. Growing up in that house meant growing up around a business where nothing was outsourced and nobody got handed off. Every client got the same two people from the first call to the closing table.

That is the standard I measure my own projects against. If I put something on the internet with my name on it, I want to be the person who answers the email when it breaks.

Where I am now

I am a business freshman at the University of Nevada, Reno. I picked Reno on purpose. It is far enough from Las Vegas that I had to build a life rather than inherit one, and it is a real city with a real downtown, a university core of roughly 24,000 students, and a business community small enough that a person can actually get a meeting. Vegas taught me how to build for tourists. Reno is teaching me how to build for a place people live.

Business is the right major for me because it sits exactly where I already spend my time — between the technology and the reason anyone would pay for it. I am not interested in code as a puzzle. I am interested in the moment when someone uses a thing I built and it saves them an hour, and in whether that hour is worth paying for.

What I build

Three products, and all of them are live. Two I started myself. The third is a company that hired me to redesign what its users actually see.

GoMojo — an AI concierge for Las Vegas

Vegas is not a city with too few options. It is a city with too many. You land at 4 PM with one night, two hundred restaurants, eighty shows, and a partner who says "I don't know, you pick." GoMojo answers that. You tell it your vibe and your budget, and about six seconds later you have a complete three-stop night — real venues, real times, real costs — plus hidden gems, happy hours that are live right now, and a Midway Meetup tool that finds the halfway point when you are meeting a friend across town.

What makes it work is not one chatbot. It is a swarm of seven specialized agents that argue before they answer: one guards the budget, one matches the aesthetic, one sanity-checks distance and open hours, one remembers what you have saved, one calibrates romance versus squad versus solo, one checks tonight's calendar, and one knows which Tuesday spots are actually busy on a Tuesday. You see the plan. You do not see the argument.

The part I am proudest of is not the AI. It is that I built the whole platform — app, agents, partner system, QR co-branded landing pages, multi-city architecture — for under a thousand dollars, total. That number is the reason the free tier can stay genuinely free and the paid tier can stay at five dollars. Low costs are not a bragging right. They are a design constraint that keeps you honest.

TagData — the redesign I was brought in to lead

TagData is a personal data vault. It holds the records that define you across eight life domains — health, employment, finance, insurance, vehicle, property, education — and its centerpiece is the TagData ID: a QR share card that hands over exactly the slice you choose and nothing more. The shared data lives inside the link itself, so it never touches a server. That is not a privacy policy you have to trust. It is a design that makes the promise structurally true.

I did not start TagData. I was hired to recreate its frontend, and given full authority over the redesign — at a company with $3.5 million invested behind it. That is a lot of rope to hand someone, and it is the work I am proudest of so far. Not because the code was hard, but because every screen had to answer to one design language instead of accumulating into a pile of one-off pages.

What that looked like day to day: a token-driven design system where every color, size and easing resolves to a single source of truth; a dark mode built as its own palette rather than an inverted copy of the light one; and the discipline to make the share card degrade gracefully when a payload exceeds what a scannable QR code can physically hold. Constraints like that are where the real design decisions live.

GoInvest — eight investors who disagree with each other

GoInvest is a multi-agent research sandbox. Eight AI personas modeled on real investing philosophies — macro momentum, technical discipline, pure quant, risk parity, growth-at-a-reasonable-price, a biotech policy specialist, a drug-delivery briefer, and a forensic short-seller whose whole job is to attack the thesis — debate a single stock, vote, and size a position. Every debate, trade and outcome is logged so the record can be checked later.

Two rules are hard-coded and non-negotiable: it trades on paper only, and every user-facing screen says it is educational and not investment advice. Building something that touches money taught me more about restraint than about finance. The interesting engineering was not making the agents smart. It was making sure a confident agent could not do damage.

Where I have actually worked

The products are the interesting part of my week. They are not the part that has taught me the most about customers.

  • Nevada Premier Valet — I park and retrieve cars for a living right now. You are handed someone's most expensive object, in the dark, in a hurry, and the entire job is that they never once have to worry about it.
  • Pressure washing, self-employed — I wash driveways, walkways and back patios for houses that are about to be inspected and sold. I find the work, quote it, do it, and get paid for it. It is the closest thing I have to a business with no software in it, and it is where I learned that showing up when you said you would is most of the product.
  • Lee Canyon — a summer on the grill at the ski area up in the Spring Mountains. A hot line at lunch rush is the best operations class I have taken. Everything is a queue, everything has a bottleneck, and the bottleneck is never where you assumed.
  • Maggiano's Little Italy — nine months in a full-service restaurant through the end of 2025. Busy dining rooms teach you to read a table in about three seconds, which turns out to be the same skill as reading a user.

None of these are on my resume because they are impressive. They are on it because they are true, and because service work is where you learn that the customer does not care how hard the thing was to build.

How I work

  • Ship first, polish second
  • Design systems, not pages
  • Constraints beat budgets
  • Log everything
  • Read the docs
  • Answer your own support email

I build fast because I would rather have something running badly on a real server than something perfect in a document. A live thing gives you feedback. A plan gives you opinions. But shipping fast only works if you are strict somewhere else, so I am strict about design tokens, about logging, and about never letting a system take an action a human would not have approved.
